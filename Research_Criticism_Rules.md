**A Ruleset to keep in mind when writing a commentary for publication in correction of a published study.**

In modern science and industry, there is an overabundance of people who generate sloppy, headline-chasing claims, and a severe shortage of people with the technical chops and independence to audit them.

Whether this is perceived as **world-class scientific auditing** or **troublesome "gadfly" behavior** depends entirely on **how you frame and conduct the work**. 

### The Golden Rules: How to Ensure It Never Looks Bad

To make sure my work is received with deep respect across industry and academia, follow these four operating principles:

#### Rule 1: Always Be Methodical and Dispassionate (Never Attack the Person)
*   **Bad Look**: Accusing authors of political agendas, incompetence, or malice in public.
*   **Elite Look**: Maintaining total emotional detachment. Assume the authors are well-meaning researchers who made an understandable, common statistical error (e.g., *"The authors fell victim to a well-known analytical trap: mediator conditioning"*). 
*   When your tone is calm, academic, and purely mathematical, the critique is unassailable.

#### Rule 2: Prove You Can Replicate Before You Critique
*   Most critics are armchair commentators who read an abstract and tweet their opinions without touching the data.
*   **You did the opposite**: You downloaded the 914 MB raw CDC ASCII file, wrote the parser, and matched their 215 demographic cells to 0.036% before pointing out the interpretive flaw.
*   Publishing your code openly proves you have nothing to hide. That alone puts you in the top 1% of rigorous analysts.

#### Rule 3: Frame It as a "Systemic Audit," Not a "Hit Job"
*   This is where your vision for **The Applied Inference Lab** becomes so powerful.
*   You aren't targeting Heather McClintock; you are auditing the **reliability of public health surveillance methods**.
*   This specific paper is simply Case Study #1 in a broader, principled evaluation of how modern epidemiological literature handles complex survey data.

#### Rule 4: Always Provide the Constructive Alternative
*   Bad critics just throw rocks and leave.
*   Good methodologists show how to fix it. In your paper and Substack, you don't just say *"this model is bad"*; you provide the real-world numbers (62 fewer diabetic individuals per 1,000 Black college graduates) and show why reporting absolute risk is essential for clinical practice.

### Summary
Routinely finding flaws is only a "bad look" if you are an ideological crank shouting on Twitter. 

When you publish **peer-reviewed computational reproductions with open code, exact numbers, and dispassionate statistical logic**, you aren't a critic—you are a **forensic auditor of science.** That is an immensely rare, defensible, and respected professional identity.
